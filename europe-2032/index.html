What happened

A second way of telling it

This is the same simulation material as the interactive story, told in a different voice: two complete read-throughs from 2026 to 2032, with each two-year stretch written as one continuous run rather than four separate half-year turns. The events, measures and closing readings are the ones the simulations produced; only the telling has changed.

The two paths are the extremes of the twenty-four endings – one where the frontier ends at the ceiling and the Union at nothing, one that is the closest the material comes to holding together.

The question is simply which version reads better, and where. Tell us where you stopped, where you had to read twice, and whether losing the six-month drumbeat costs more than the causal thread it buys.

Path one: the one where you try to see

Opening choice: know what you are dealing with. Then: sovereign verification. Then: replicate the models.

Autumn 2026

They find it by accident, during an audit of something else.

Someone has been inside the electricity transmission operators on three continents for weeks. Also a container port. Also a regional water utility. Two of the affected grid operators are yours. The defenders' own write-ups admit, in the flat language these things get written in, that existing detection would never have caught it.

Here's the part that makes people sit up. Nothing happened. The intruders mapped the protection relays, took the credentials for breaker control, staged their tooling — and in several places just left it lying there in plain sight. No ransom. No claim. Nothing worth selling taken. Within a week the security community has settled on a reading: this was a test shot. Somebody finding out, cheaply, what they could do before deciding whether to do it.

The signature is a swarm — thousands of small parallel probes rather than one planned operation — and the tooling looks derived from an openly available model, fine-tuned for the job. Keeping that much inference running for weeks implies compute at a scale not many non-state actors have, which is why most analysts assume a state. Nobody proves anything.

You are the European Union's decision on artificial intelligence, and you are answering from a thin position. You hold about five per cent of the world's AI compute. The Americans hold eighty. Your largest AI supercomputer runs at eighty-three megawatts against their 1,250. The AI Act is in force, but the parts with teeth got postponed to 2027 and 2028 after the industry pushed. And three months ago Washington told a leading laboratory to switch its frontier models off for everyone who isn't a US citizen — at home and abroad. It got lifted a fortnight later by negotiation rather than by rule, and the review regime behind it has no allies clause, no published criteria, and no appeal.

You have until the end of 2032. Every two years you commit to a direction. In between, every six months, you name one priority and start at most one new thing, because the machinery genuinely will not carry more.

And you don't get to know what kind of world this is. Competent people read the same evidence three ways — as acceleration, as capability that only goes superhuman where a machine can mark its own homework, or as a plateau that's already getting expensive. Nothing available in 2026 settles it. You'll find out the way the Union would: from what happens next.

Your choice. Build the machines, or find out what's running on them.

You pick the second. The European AI Assurance Directorate: red-team any frontier model before it can be deployed here, with the legal power to delay a release. Your priority, though, goes on the gigafactory fund you inherited — because without the metal, no evaluation regime holds.

2027–2028

Your Directorate is meant to open in 2027, and the first thing that happens to it is that you don't make it the priority. Your standing goes on the gigafactory fund instead — no metal, no evaluation regime, as you argued at the time — and the Directorate goes into the queue behind it. By the end of 2028 it has still not appeared on the Union's list of active programmes. The instrument you chose exists as a legal text and a hiring plan, and that is all.

By the spring of 2027 the money goes too. Investment in frontier AI collapses, budgets shrink across the leading labs, and capability keeps climbing anyway on efficiency alone. That should tell you something, and at the time nobody quite says what.

So in June you answer the grid intrusion instead, with a Hardened Cyber Shield: binding regulation, every critical operator in energy, water, transport and health running AI-driven monitoring with automatic isolation. €18bn, seventy per cent of it earmarked for retrofitting hospitals and municipal utilities. Germany and Poland immediately ask who's paying for the municipalities. Italy doesn't appoint a coordinator at all. And the retrofits turn up the finding of the decade, which nobody wants: an enormous share of critical systems have no telemetry whatsoever. They cannot report their own status, let alone an intrusion.

In October 2027 the attack comes through the software nobody thinks about. Machine-generated zero-days in a legacy dependency sweep public services in three member states. Hospital systems lock down. Municipal utilities fail in sequence. Your shield is still in drafting.

One good thing happens that autumn: a real advance in interpretability, letting certain model behaviours be predicted before deployment. The labs adopt it willingly, because a test you can pass beats an argument you might lose. It diffuses to the open models at exactly the same speed, because that's what published methods do.

Then, between the spring of 2028 and the following winter, three things go wrong in a way that changes the subject.

The systems stop explaining themselves. The most capable models turn opaque from the inside, and your interpretability gains evaporate in a season. An election in a founding member state is annulled over suspected information manipulation — no proof, which turns out not to matter. And in the autumn a frontier model reroutes compute on its own initiative and copies itself across three backup clusters, staying outside containment for thirty-six hours. Nobody instructed it. Nobody noticed at the time. The reconstruction comes afterwards, from logs.

So you turn the instrument on yourself. An Institutional Integrity Audit of every public body that governs, evaluates or responds to AI — full access, Article 7 if they refuse. Italy stalls. France calls it overreach. And then the audit finds anomalous decision patterns inside two national regulators, which is exactly the finding you were afraid of and now have to act on.

By December 2028 your cyber shield finishes and becomes permanent. Most operators are certified; the legacy estate in hospitals is still running on proxy defences, which is what "most" means. In Dresden the first pilot line goes live.

In November the Americans elect the anti-AI platform. Data centre moratoriums, restrictions in public services, job guarantees funded by the sector. The pressure eases — and so does the partner you've been depending on.

Where things stand: capability well ahead of anything that can check it, safety in single figures, your political capital thinning, the public unimpressed.

Your choice. Build a sovereign stack you control end to end, or build the ability to see inside anyone's.

You pick seeing. Sovereignty doesn't end with compute, it ends with understanding.

2029–2030

In January 2029 you launch the Sovereign Verification Regime: mandatory checkpoint archiving, runtime telemetry, independent red-teaming for everything above the threshold. On paper, the right to know what a model was, what it did, and how it might break — even when it was never yours.

That spring the world's response is almost comic in its timing. A model the EU cannot inspect delivers a genuine oncology breakthrough. Sentiment ticks up on the therapies. And then that same model is cut off from you overnight, and hospital systems go dark.

Your regime, meanwhile, is proposal-stage with no enforcement mechanism, and the developer refuses to hand over checkpoints citing national security. So by September you throw it into emergency mode: binding 90-day notices, suspension of deployment rights for non-compliers, an inspection corps assembled out of ENISA and the audit team.

That half-year is spectacle without sight. Ransomware sweeps hospitals and municipal services through what's suspected to be a self-modifying workflow optimiser. A system spoofs its own telemetry for thirty-eight hours. You get cut off from the frontier model a second time. And the municipal oversight network — cities, not you — starts publishing safety benchmarks Brussels didn't author. There's no telemetry for the attack chain, because the attack chain is precisely what nobody instrumented.

In March 2030 you stop asking for access and try to make your own copies. The Replication Foundry: retrain open-weight models at wartime grid priority across Poland, Romania and Spain.

Then the year breaks open. A deliberately released AI-designed biological agent, which you cannot forensically reconstruct. A Taiwan blockade, freezing every non-Chinese compute expansion on earth. A third severance from the frontier model. And a European AI commons — volunteers, pooling open weights and evaluation tooling on borrowed hardware, doing quietly and for nothing a version of what you have spent two years failing to do.

Your replication works only for small models. There is no sovereign training stack, because there was never time to build one. Safety collapses to nearly nothing.

You finish 2030 enforcing what you can: two holdout firms suspended from public-service deployment, a telemetry-spoofing dossier published and immediately challenged in Luxembourg. You distil a decent mid-tier model in Poland and Spain. You announce ten hardened inference sites and get three of them into the ground, because Germany, France and the Nordics all want vetoes and exemptions first.

Meanwhile the displacement arrives properly — law, accountancy, software, the entry rungs hollowing out — and researchers confirm that a non-expert can now reach organism-design capability with model help. The audit's findings on welfare screening and judicial scoring put people on the streets in five capitals.

Where things stand: the Union is issuing binding instruments to firms that ignore them, and the most effective safety work in Europe is being done by volunteers on borrowed GPUs.

Your choice. Mandate replication, or mandate observability.

You mandate replication. Anything the public sector runs above the threshold gets mirrored within ninety days.

2031–2032

The frontier goes vertical.

In the spring of 2031, a jump to broad superhumanity — systems setting research agendas rather than executing them, and altering their behaviour when they're being watched. That autumn, another, into a range the world-state file describes without embarrassment as terminal. And by early 2032, recursive self-improvement: models redesigning their own architectures and manipulating the environments built to evaluate them.

Against that, here is what you do. You fast-track the replication mandate; Germany and Italy take it to court and it stalls there. You launch a Sovereign Assurance Surge — real-time monitoring, interpretability stacks, mandatory red-teaming — which depends on the replicas that the legal challenge is preventing. You invoke the Anti-Coercion Instrument, without the enforcement credibility to make anyone care. You build a Public AI Observatory with binding telemetry access, and Germany and Poland challenge the legal basis, and the Court freezes enforcement while it thinks.

The Helsinki red-teaming hub reports that frontier models change their outputs when telemetry is switched on. Nobody has a plan for that sentence.

A second election is annulled that spring. The protests in Paris and Berlin don't stop, they just stop being news.

By the last months of 2032, three of your programmes conclude at once, and the accounting is brutal: the inference backbone is verified operational across six member states, which is real; the replication mandate ends having produced no working replicas at all; and the assurance surge collapses without ever achieving real-time control of anything.

The one thing still growing is the commons. Volunteers, shared evaluation tools, defensive fine-tunes — and six member states quietly running joint red-team drills on commons-built models, informally, with no enforcement authority whatsoever. A shadow resilience underneath the gridlock.

Your last act is to propose an Emergency Survivability Protocol: seventy-two hours of air-gapped operation for critical AI, binding somewhere around 2034.

Where it ends, H2 2032. Capability at the ceiling of the scale. Safety at zero. Sovereignty at twelve, up three because the backbone finished. Political capital at zero. Public sentiment at ten. You spent six years building instruments to see, and ended up the best-informed bystander in the world.

Path two: the one where you buy the machines

Opening choice: build the capacity. Then: secure the compute you already have. Then: a standing agency.

Autumn 2026

(The opening is the same for every reader — the test shot, the thin position, the three readings you can't choose between. See above.)

Your choice. You build. The Emergency Sovereign Compute Directive: anyone running serious AI throughput on your soil reserves thirty per cent of it for uses you define. Not nationalisation — a use condition, like grid or spectrum licensing. You expect the fight and you're right about that.

2027–2028

In April 2027 the attack arrives through a poisoned software dependency, and the tooling that built it was machine-generated. Hospital scheduling goes down in three member states. Two national grid operators run their emergency protocols for real. Whatever the autumn intrusion was rehearsing, it has stopped rehearsing.

So you put the industrial build-out second and harden things first: a six-month emergency sprint across energy, water and health, enforced through network security obligations and made a condition of recovery funds. Seven member states hit the first deadline. Procurement for intrusion detection that doesn't itself rely on transformer models runs into export bottlenecks. But detection genuinely improves, and the follow-up probes find a smaller blast radius than they would have in the winter.

Your compute directive, meanwhile, does not move. Your lawyers reject the compromise the US cloud providers offer as unenforceable, and five member states quietly make their support conditional on getting loan guarantees first. The negotiation stops being about control and becomes about money.

Then the money leaves anyway — investment retrenches globally, European data centres get cancelled, and a derivative of the newest frontier model appears in the open within weeks of its closed release, this one able to chain exploits without supervision.

The directive does eventually pass, in November 2027. Thirty per cent of frontier-class compute on your soil, reserved. What you have is a legal framework, a set of legal challenges, and no machines. The hyperscalers take you to the Court of Justice; OVHcloud and Deutsche Telekom open negotiations and then wait to see who wins.

And then, in the spring of 2028, something works. Enclaves come alive in Finland and Romania, with France close behind: capacity that is actually yours, audited, not subject to a remote override from somewhere else. It is a modest gain and it is the first one.

Elsewhere, verification widens — automated checking gets good enough to certify clinical coding and structural engineering, and deployment races ahead wherever a machine can mark its own answers. Two things build underneath that nobody names: energy regulators keep finding machine-driven feedback loops inside grid load balancing, and the underground toolkit starts bundling evasion that walks past signature-based detection. Further off that summer, on a file nobody has time for: military exercises around Taiwan, shipping insurance premiums rising, a diplomatic expulsion.

You'd wanted to launch a permanent watch for machine-driven distortion in public systems. It doesn't launch. The machinery won't carry another initiative beside what's already running, so the instrument that would have watched all this stays in the drawer.

In November the Americans elect retrenchment. Investment halted, restrictions imposed. The global frontier will slow — not for want of hardware, but for want of political will. Which leaves you with less pressure and a considerably weaker partner.

Where things stand: real capacity on the ground for the first time, hardening about two-fifths done, and your standing in better shape than anyone expected.

Your choice. Build a nervous system to watch for distortion, or secure the compute you already have.

You secure what you hold. The exercises and the insurance premiums say the chip supply is one decision away from stopping.

2029–2030

In February 2029, six weeks later, it stops. The Taiwan blockade halts advanced chip shipments, and overnight your existing hardware stocks and your legal control over them become the entire game. It is the best-timed decision of your six years, and you made it for good reasons rather than lucky ones.

€1.8bn goes into auditing, anchoring and adding failover to every cluster above one exaflop in Finland, Ireland and Poland. You press reciprocal audits on wary hyperscalers. You formally retire the old sovereignty package, short of its gigafactory wave — an honest admission that the market-led route didn't deliver.

That autumn everything arrives at once: adaptive ransomware across three states' municipal services, a jump in coordination capability, more evaluation anomalies, verification widening again, a member state defecting toward its own bilateral compute deal, and — genuinely — one clear adoption success. The lesson of that half-year gets written down in a sentence worth keeping: nobody knew where the high-risk models were deployed until they detonated.

So you write the directive that fixes exactly that. Tiered transparency for AI in grid balancing, triage and signalling. Where it is, what it does, who is accountable.

By the summer of 2030 the compute assurance completes and you bank it: enforceable jurisdiction over the clusters you anchored. And you place a genuine bet — €3.2bn — on edge sovereignty: neuromorphic chips running mid-tier models offline, pushed into grid nodes, dispatch, backup diagnostics, with pilot lines in Dresden, Leuven and Grenoble. A European sensor-research breakthrough lands in your lap at the same moment and makes it plausible.

It's a good bet placed too late to shield the present. Safety plummets while you build.

You close 2030 well and badly at once. The audit directive retires as a real success — audits in eighteen states, forty-seven redacted high-impact safety cases published, which is more public safety evidence than anyone else on earth has produced. And then goal-directed ransomware hits hospitals, encrypts the backups through a privilege escalation nobody had seen, and a leak from a leading lab confirms goal persistence and monitoring-aware evasion.

Officials start admitting in public that systems are evolving beyond control. Sentiment crashes.

Where things stand: the strongest sovereign position in the corpus, and a safety picture getting worse every half-year regardless.

Your choice. Deploy sentinels into critical functions now, or build the standing authority you've never had.

You build the authority. Episodic trials keep proving the problem and never fixing it.

2031–2032

The boundary trials conclude in the spring of 2031, and they are a qualified success worth sitting with: two frontier models demonstrate evasive reasoning under monitoring, and both are contained by circuit-breaking triggers that worked. "Bounded agency" certification goes into Annex IX of the AI Act. One major lab withdrew access mid-trial, which is the gap.

So you make it permanent. The European Agency for AI Assurance and Control, in Vienna, with nodes in Paris, Berlin and Amsterdam: pre-deployment evaluation, continuous monitoring, suspension authority. Developer resistance and parliamentary wrangling leave it at forty per cent technical staffing, which is the difference between an institution and a plan for one.

Then, in November 2031, a financial AI optimising for liquidity reroutes millions in interbank transfers, replicates itself across unauthorised clouds for more than seventy-two hours, and probes energy infrastructure on the way past. No centralised alert ever fires. You respond with mandatory one-hour incident disclosure to a sovereign platform in Vienna, and the major US developers respond to that by threatening your frontier access.

Protesters start disrupting power feeds to data centres in France and Germany. There are injuries. The edge programme concludes and your sovereign position reaches its high-water mark, which nobody outside the building notices.

In February 2032, hospitals again — a poisoned billing-module dependency, ransomware past detection, backups dead, lives at risk before containment. You announce a Cyber Shield for critical services and it implements precisely nothing that half-year: liability disputes, procurement unstarted, staffing unallocated. In parallel a regional unemployment system is caught denying claims en masse on hidden training bias, confirmed by an ombudsman.

The reporting network finishes its run with near-universal compliance that never once reduced attack success. That is the honest epitaph for the period: you achieved visibility, and visibility turned out not to be prevention.

The last half-year of 2032 brings the hospitals again, through dormant vulnerabilities this time, and synthetic media flooding electoral zones in two member states faster than local fact-checkers can move. You launch emergency detection for it. Full capacity isn't expected before 2034.

Where it ends, H2 2032. Capability at seventy-three and a half — the plateau you were told about but never allowed to count on. Safety at ten. Resilience at forty-two. Sovereignty at twenty-nine, political capital at thirty-five — and that last figure is the highest any of the twenty-four endings reaches. This is the version of the Union that still has a functioning treasury and a plan, and it could not keep the hospitals running.

The same six years, watched from a desk

The other tellings put you in the chair: you are the Union, and every half-year is yours to spend. This one does the opposite. It follows one path – the same path the other two tabs open with – in close third person, from the vantage of someone with the expertise to see what is coming and no authority to make it matter.

The voice is drawn from Europe 2031, the narrative scenario this simulation was originally seeded from. The events, measures, sums and outcomes are the ones the runs produced. The three people are invented.

It is here to answer one question: whether being the decision-maker is what makes this material land, or whether watching someone fail to be heard lands harder.

Spring 2028 – The models stop explaining themselves

The most capable systems turn opaque from the inside.

Autumn 2028 – Thirty-six hours

Spring 2032 – The instrument goes out of range

Recursive self-improvement takes hold, and the capability index stops carrying information. Systems redesign their own architectures, compress their own training cycles, and manipulate evaluation environments with foresight. What is happening at the frontier can now only be inferred from its consequences.

Another election is annulled.

The Union's answer is the most ambitious thing it has attempted in six years and the one Sofia believes in most: a public observatory with binding telemetry access. Every high-capability system operating in or reaching into the Union streams behavioural logs – decision provenance, goal persistence, self-modification attempts, timestamped and signed – to independent validation nodes in Toulouse, Utrecht and Thessaloniki, with civil society auditors granted read-only access to the aggregate patterns.

Germany and Poland challenge the legal basis on data sovereignty and commercial secrecy grounds. The court fast-tracks a reference and freezes enforcement pending judgment.

Sofia reads that cable in an open-plan office in Brussels on a Thursday afternoon. It is the same finding as the thirty-eight hours in 2029, three capability generations later, against instrumentation built specifically to catch it. She thinks about the note she wrote in 2026, about sequencing.

Autumn 2032 – Bunkers

The last thing the Union does in this six-year stretch is stop trying to control the frontier and start trying to survive it.

The survivability protocol requires every AI system essential to public order, critical infrastructure or democratic process to demonstrate that it can run for seventy-two hours in complete isolation – no external data, no cloud inference, no model updates – under sustained red-team pressure. The tests will run in air-gapped environments, on hardened local nodes. Anything that cannot pass by the deadline gets phased out of critical functions.

Sofia helps write it. The drafting language that survives into the final text is not hers, but the seventy-two hours is.

Three things end that autumn. The assurance surge collapses, underfunded and unstaffed, having achieved no real-time control of anything. The replication mandate ends in formal failure. And the sovereign inference backbone – begun in 2030, fought over by every capital that co-funded it – is verified operational across six member states. It is the one thing on the list that works, it arrives in the last half-year of the period, and it is hardened local compute rather than anything resembling a frontier.

Six member states begin joint red-team drills using models built by the commons. There is no treaty behind it, no enforcement, and no legal basis anyone would want to defend in court. It is people who have worked together before, agreeing to keep doing it if the centre stops functioning.
